One of the first things you have to do when you arrive in the UK is ring up and book an appointment for a National Insurance number. I put that call off until I had finished an intensive English course and felt moderately confident I would get through it.

The operator asked, "Do you want to make an appointment?"

"No thanks," I said. "I'm good."

The operator replied, gently, "But you need one, so I'm going to book it."

I had given up a permanent contract driving high-speed trains in Italy to make that call. In Italy a permanent contract is the closest thing to winning the lottery that a job can give you. Nobody pushed me out. After almost a decade the work had become too repetitive, and I could see it was not a job I could do for the rest of my life. So I handed the contract back and left, with little money saved and very little English.

I've changed career twice since then: from train driver to developer, and from developer to engineering manager. I now have a lot of conversations with engineers who are worried about what AI does to their careers, and those two changes are most of what I have to offer them.

## The first one cost everything I had

I did the retraining in Italy, before I moved, while I was still driving shifts. Two bootcamps, one in web design and one in backend development with PHP and MySQL, six months each. Then a few months of intensive English, because I could not interview in a language I did not speak.

That phone call is the moment I knew it would work. Not because my English was good enough, which it plainly wasn't, but because someone on the other end was willing to carry me over the gap. All my life I had powered through on my own strength. On that call I found out that you can sometimes let go and be helped. Years later that turned into how I build trust and how I delegate. At the time I just had an appointment.

The payback was almost immediate. About six months after moving I had my first developer job, at a small Italian-run company in London that sold fashion online.

That change was hard in every direction at once: new industry, new country, new language, no safety net.

## What the railway gave me, and what I had to leave there

Driving trains can look romantic from the outside. From the inside it was nights without sleep, and cold, rain and wind while you moved trains around the depot. It was hours waiting for a train that didn't arrive, already knowing you would miss dinner with your family. It was friends slowly disappearing, because nobody can keep up with someone on long and strange shifts.

That gave me two things I still use. One is flexibility, and the resilience that comes with it. The other is a baseline. I know what a hard working life feels like, so I measure every job since against that one, and it makes a difficult quarter in software easier to keep in proportion.

One thing did not carry over, and I had to unlearn it. In the cab, being able to do everything yourself matters a great deal. In software it is the opposite.

I found that out with my first trainee. I had six months to help him succeed as a frontend developer, and my approach was simple: do as I do and you'll be fine. He was fine in the end. But I had been teaching him to become me, and that is where I learned the difference between a mentor and a coach. My job was to help him grow in his own way, not in mine.

## Always the oldest in the room

Because software is my second career, I have been the oldest person in the room for almost all of it. Every direct manager I've had was younger than me.

It was never a problem. Hierarchy should follow knowledge and merit, not age.

The one I think of first was a young frontend developer in the UK who had ended up managing all the frontend developers, without really wanting to. You could tell the age difference made him a little uneasy. He handled it very well. He was always calm, and he looked for the logical way through every conversation and every decision. I still have a very high opinion of him, and years later we met up again at a developer conference in the Netherlands.

I mention it because any career change as an adult puts you in this position: a beginner, reporting to someone younger. Once you accept that knowledge outranks age, there is nothing left to be awkward about.

## The change that didn't work

Not every move paid off. At one point I tried contracting, and it was a nightmare for me.

The human part of the job was missing. I was a billing machine: hours in, invoice out. I lasted six months, and it ended in burnout.

Burnout, for me, was dread. I couldn't afford to stop working, so I changed job and tried to recover while still employed, in the middle of the pandemic. I went through a couple of short roles, six to nine months each. I couldn't focus. My concentration was close to non-existent, and every task looked like a mountain I had no way of climbing.

What I remember most is the collision. I was raised to believe that how you behave at work matters, and that you always do it properly. Every morning that belief ran straight into the dread of having to go.

It told me what I can't work without, which is people.

## The second one was easier, and that matters

Moving from engineer to engineering manager was not easy, but it was much easier than the first change. The industry stayed the same. The way of working stayed the same. I had to drop some habits I'd built as an engineer and add new ones, mostly around organisation and communication.

The hardest habit to drop was proposing solutions. As a specialist that had been my entire job: someone brings a problem, I bring the answer. As a manager I am not the specialist any more. My duty now is to listen to the people who are.

It was the lesson of the phone call and the trainee a third time. Each change asked me to let go of doing it myself.

This is the comparison I'd offer an engineer worried about AI. What's in front of most of us looks far more like my second change than my first. You are not starting again in a new country with a new language. You keep the industry, the instincts and the understanding of how systems break, and you change what you do with them.

## What engineers are actually afraid of

I have a lot of these conversations, and almost nobody I talk to is afraid of AI itself.

They are afraid of leaders who use it to reach their own goals. AI is a convenient reason to give for cuts. What that reason leaves out is that there was usually another way to hit the same target without damaging people's lives. AI becomes the scapegoat, and engineers watch that happen across the industry every day.

So the fear is reasonable, and I don't try to talk anyone out of it. My two career changes were my decision. I picked the timing and I carried the risk. An engineer whose role is cut "because of AI" chose none of it, and telling them to simply reinvent themselves skips over that.

## What I tell them

I tell them that this is what our market does. It has changed like this before and it will again.

When I started, frontend meant jQuery, and most of the CSS I wrote was inline, on legacy services. Look at frontend now. It is not the same job, and the people who stayed in it adapted.

AI is a bigger shift than that one, and our jobs will change again because of it. But adaptation is our strength.

So I keep two things apart when I talk to someone. The technology changing the work is normal, and you can prepare for it. A leader using the technology as cover for a cut is a choice that person made, and no amount of retraining on your side makes it your fault.

Recently an IT professional told me they wanted to become a junior developer, and asked what to do. I had one suggestion: forget about writing code for now. Writing code matters, but it is not the first step. The first step is understanding what your strong points are.

Then build the mindset, because that is the biggest strength you can have. A developer does not write code. That is a small part of the task. The real task is to break a problem down into pieces that can be acted on and fitted into a plan, and to connect what a customer needs with what the technology can do.

Holding that much context is something I think AI is a long way from doing. If you can do it well, and show that you can, it is the closest thing to job security I know of in this industry.

## I'm worried too

I'd be lying if I left this part out. My own role is going to change drastically in the next few years, and preparing for it is hard for two reasons. Nobody knows exactly how it will change. And at this level there is very little time set aside for training.

What I can do is work out the most plausible way it changes, and prepare for that. My bet is this. The focus on people becomes more important than it was. So do delivery and quality. And my technical skills are the part that is easiest to replace: an engineer who knows how to use AI properly can cover most of what I bring there.

That last one is not comfortable to write down. It is also what the contracting months showed me years ago: the part of the work I can't do without is the people. If my bet is right, it is also the part the job will need most.

So I've decided where my time goes. More of it into developing people, and into making delivery better. On top of that I bring my experience in product-oriented development, which makes me useful at the discovery and planning stage. That is something AI, at least for now, cannot do.

I don't have a tidy answer. I have twice gone from knowing my job to not knowing it, and both times the not knowing was temporary.

### Your next step

Write down what your job consisted of in your first year in this industry, then cross out everything you no longer do. You have already got through all of that change. Then write down the one part you would refuse to give up, the way I found out I couldn't give up working with people. Build the next change around keeping that.
