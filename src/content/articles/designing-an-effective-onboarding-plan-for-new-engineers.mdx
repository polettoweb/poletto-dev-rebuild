First impressions matter. When a new software engineer joins your team, they're a mix of excited and anxious - they want to prove themselves, understand the systems, and start contributing.

Many engineering organisations treat onboarding as an afterthought: a chaotic first week of requesting permissions, waiting for hardware, and reading outdated documentation. A poor onboarding experience doesn't just delay productivity; it erodes confidence and increases early turnover. An effective plan is structured, proactive, and phased - it reduces cognitive overload while giving the new hire clear, actionable milestones.

## Pre-boarding: before day one

Onboarding begins the moment the candidate signs the offer letter. The goal is to eliminate administrative friction so the engineer can focus on learning from hour one: hardware configured and delivered ahead of time, accounts pre-provisioned for GitHub, Slack, Jira, and cloud access, an onboarding buddy assigned - a peer, not their manager, who's the go-to for the questions that feel too small to ask - and a welcome email a few days out describing what to expect and who they'll meet.

## Week one: orientation and the first-day commit

The first week is about building familiarity and psychological safety, not architecture documents. One of the best ways to boost a new engineer's confidence is to help them ship a change to production within their first 24 to 48 hours - as small as fixing a typo or adding their name to a team directory. It validates that their environment is set up, their permissions are correct, and it demystifies the deployment pipeline immediately.

Alongside that: a 1:1 with the manager to clarify the team's mission and what success looks like in the first month, a session with the buddy to set up the local environment and update any outdated docs in the process, and an invitation to every regular ceremony - standups, refinement, retros - as an observer.

## The first 30 days: context and collaboration

By week two or three, the focus shifts from setup to understanding the system and collaborating. Pair programming with different team members exposes the new hire to different coding styles and domain knowledge faster than solo ramp-up ever could. Assign small-to-medium tasks that touch different parts of the codebase, schedule short architecture deep-dives with domain experts, and ask directly what was confusing about the onboarding so far - then actually update the docs.

## Day 30 to 90: autonomy and ownership

This is where the new hire moves from student to active contributor. Assign a small, end-to-end feature they own - gathering requirements, writing the design doc, implementing, and managing the deployment. Encourage them to meet product, design, and support directly rather than through their manager. Run formal 30-60-90 day checkpoints to discuss progress and close any gaps in knowledge or expectations.

## A checklist worth stealing

```
Pre-boarding
- Hardware ordered, delivery date confirmed
- Accounts created, buddy assigned

Week 1
- First-day commit shipped
- Local build working, ceremonies attended as observer

Month 1
- 2-3 minor bug fixes or doc tasks closed
- Paired with 3+ team members, architecture reviewed with the tech lead

Months 2-3
- End-to-end feature owned and delivered
- 60-day and 90-day reviews complete
```

### Your next step

A great onboarding experience doesn't happen by accident - it's the result of intentional planning and continuous improvement. Look at your last new hire's first week honestly: was there a first-day commit, or did they spend it waiting on IT? Fix that one gap before the next person joins.
