Nobody announces that a promotion into management went wrong. The team does it for them - quietly, in ways that show up months before it ever reaches a dashboard.

And here's the uncomfortable part: when a new manager struggles, it's almost never because they're a bad person, or even a bad leader. It's because we promoted them for the wrong reasons, and then looked away.

I've made this mistake. I've also spent years learning to spot it early, because the cost of *not* spotting it isn't abstract - it's measured in the good people who quietly leave. Here are the signs.

## 1. They're still the best engineer on the team

The team goes to them *for code* and routes around them *for decisions*. They kept the hardest tickets because handing them off felt like dropping the ball. Six months in, they're a fantastic senior engineer with a manager's title and a calendar they resent. I've called this [the critical-path manager](/blog/what-i-look-for-when-hiring-and-growing-engineering-managers/) - the most common failure mode there is, and a comfortable one, because it *looks* like dedication.

## 2. The team has gone quiet

Fewer questions in standup. Fewer half-formed ideas floated. Fewer disagreements. It's easy to read quiet as harmony - *finally, a drama-free team*. It's usually the opposite. **Silence is the sound of people who've decided it's not worth being heard.** A healthy team is a little noisy. When a team goes still under a new manager, that's not peace. That's withdrawal, and it's expensive.

## 3. Everything routes through them

No decision moves without their sign-off. It feels like diligence; it's a bottleneck with a pulse. The quickest test: when they take a week off, does the team keep moving - or does it hold its breath until they're back?

## 4. They still say "my work," not "the team's"

Listen to the pronouns in a 1:1. A manager who's made the shift talks about what the *team* shipped and names who did the hard parts. One who hasn't still measures the week by their own output - and feels a low, nagging guilt on the weeks they didn't produce anything "real." That guilt is the [identity shift](/blog/how-i-grow-an-engineer-into-a-manager/) failing to land.

## 5. Their best people are leaving - and they're surprised

Attrition is the lagging indicator. The *surprise* is the real signal. A manager who's close to their team feels disengagement building months before anyone resigns. By the time it shows up in your attrition numbers, you're two quarters too late to fix it.

## 6. Under pressure, they protect themselves

When a deadline slips or leadership pushes down, watch which way the pressure flows. A good manager absorbs it and translates it into something the team can act on. A struggling one passes it straight through untouched - or, worse, passes the blame *down*. That's someone still an individual contributor wearing a title.

## The uncomfortable pivot: it's usually not their fault

Read those six signs again, and notice something. Almost every one traces back to *how the promotion happened* - not to the person. We promoted our strongest engineer as a reward. We never asked if they wanted to manage. We didn't make the identity shift explicit, so they clung to the keyboard. We didn't build a real Staff track, so "up" only ever meant "management."

## What to actually do

Not "fire them." That's lazy, and usually wrong. Instead:

- **Diagnose honestly.** Is this a *skills* gap (coachable), a *support* gap (yours to fix), or a *fit* gap (they don't want the job)? Those need completely different responses.
- **Reopen the exit ramp, without shame.** Moving back to a Staff/IC track is a *good outcome*, not a demotion. Make that a door, not a cliff.
- **If they want it, coach the one concrete behaviour** - not "get better at management," but the specific thing: stop taking the critical-path tickets; run 1:1s that aren't status updates; absorb pressure instead of forwarding it.
- **Fix the system that created it.** A real IC ladder. Trying the job before the title. Skip that, and you'll be right back here in a year with a different name.

## The bottom line

The wrong person in a management role is rarely a villain and rarely obvious on paper. It shows up in a team that's gone quiet, a manager who never left the keyboard, and good people leaving with a polite explanation. Spot it early, be honest about the cause - and remember that the fix usually starts with the process that made the promotion, not the person who accepted it.

### Your next step

Think of one manager you promoted in the last year. This week, ask their team a single question in your own skip-level or 1:1s: *"When you're stuck, or you disagree with a decision, what happens?"* The answer will tell you - fast - whether you promoted a manager, or just handed a great engineer a harder job and a worse week.
