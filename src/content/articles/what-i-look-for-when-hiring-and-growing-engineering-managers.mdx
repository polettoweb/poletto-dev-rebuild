The most expensive hire I can make isn't a staff engineer. It's an engineering manager.

Get a senior IC hire wrong and you lose a seat of output for a few months. Get an EM hire wrong and you lose the output of an entire team - slowly, invisibly, for a year - while your best engineers quietly update their CVs. By the time the lagging indicators show up in your attrition dashboard, the damage is already compounding.

So when I hire or promote an engineering manager, I'm not evaluating whether someone is a strong engineer "ready for the next step." I'm evaluating whether they can become the *operating environment* for a group of people I care about. Those are very different questions, and conflating them is how good companies end up with a layer of managers who are really just senior engineers with calendars full of meetings.

## 1. They optimise for the team's output, not their own

The hardest transition from engineer to manager is the identity shift: your value is no longer what *you* ship. In interviews, this shows up in the pronouns. Ask a candidate about a project they're proud of. A strong IC tells you what they built. A strong manager tells you what the *team* built and names the people who did the hard parts.

The failure mode I screen hardest for is the **critical-path manager** - the EM who is still the best engineer on the team and secretly likes it that way. It feels heroic. It is actually a bottleneck wearing a cape.

## 2. They create clarity instead of consuming it

Most organisational pain is a clarity problem in disguise. A good manager is a *clarity pump*: ambiguity goes in, and clear ownership and next steps come out. I probe this with reorg questions: "Tell me about a time the roadmap changed underneath your team." The weak answer is a story about how leadership was chaotic. The strong answer is a story about how the manager [absorbed the noise](/blog/leading-in-the-fog/) and protected the team's focus while it resolved.

## 3. They can hold a technical bar without holding the keyboard

There's a lazy dichotomy in our industry: either you're "still technical" or you've "gone into management." I don't buy it. The best engineering managers I know are deeply technical - they just express it differently, by setting the constraints inside which good code gets written rather than writing it themselves. When I'm growing an IC into the role, I give them ownership of technical *governance* - the RFC process, the quality bar - before I give them a large headcount.

## 4. They treat people development as the actual job

The signal that separates a manager who *scales* from one who merely copes: they get visibly energised by other people growing. I ask every candidate, "Tell me about someone you helped level up." Some people don't really have one. Others light up and walk me through exactly where the person was stuck, what worked, and where that person is now.

## How I grow ICs into the role

Hiring externally is only half the pipeline. A few principles that have held up:

- **Test the role before you title it.** Give a slice of real management surface area - owning onboarding, running the RFC process, mentoring a junior - and watch whether they're drawn to it or drained by it.
- **Make the identity shift explicit.** New managers relapse into IC work under pressure because shipping code is where they feel competent. Name this out loud, early and repeatedly.
- **Give them guardrails, not scripts.** The principles, and room to find their own style inside them.
- **Protect them from becoming the critical path.** Actively redistribute the dependency, even when it slows delivery in the short term.

## The bottom line

Hiring engineering managers is the highest-leverage, highest-variance decision I make as a leader. The market is full of strong engineers who will make mediocre managers, and quieter people who will quietly make everyone around them better. My whole job is to tell them apart, then build the environment where the good ones can grow.

### Your next step

If you're an engineer wondering whether management is for you: does other people's growth energise you as much as your own commits used to? If yes, we should talk. If no, that's not a failure - it's a Staff-track career waiting to happen.
