In the modern technology landscape, change is the only constant. One week your squad is fully aligned on a multi-quarter roadmap; the next, a sudden pivot, an organisational restructure, or a round of layoffs throws everything into disarray.

When the organisational fog rolls in, engineering velocity stalls. Slack channels hum with speculation, productivity drops as anxiety rises, and developers begin eyeing the exit. As an engineering manager, you're caught directly in the middle - you are the shock absorber. How you handle these periods determines whether your team fractures under pressure or emerges more cohesive and focused.

## Why teams freeze

To lead effectively during a reorg, it helps to understand the response to uncertainty. Dr. David Rock's **SCARF model** - which I've also drawn on when thinking about [how metrics affect behaviour](/blog/measuring-velocity-without-killing-morale/) - explains how our brains evaluate social threats and rewards. Organisational uncertainty triggers a threat response across nearly every domain: *Will my project be cancelled? Do I have any say in what I work on next? Where do I fit in the new org chart?*

When certainty is stripped away, the brain enters a defensive threat state, and the prefrontal cortex - which governs creative problem-solving and logical reasoning - is compromised. Developers literally lose the cognitive capacity to write clean architecture because they're preoccupied with survival. Your goal isn't to pretend the fog doesn't exist. It's to reduce the threat state by injecting clarity and psychological safety wherever possible.

## The manager as a shock absorber

The most common mistake EMs make during a reorg is passing organisational stress straight down to the team, to justify why things are chaotic. That's a failure of leadership. A good manager absorbs the high-frequency organisational noise, filters out the speculation, and delivers low-frequency, actionable context to the team. That doesn't mean lying or hiding the truth - it means translating corporate volatility into stable, manageable work scopes so your developers can focus on what they do best.

## Strategies for leading through the storm

- **Define the boundaries of truth.** When information is scarce, rumours fill the vacuum. State clearly what's confirmed ("our team is staying together, but our reporting line is shifting"), be honest about what's unconfirmed ("I expect to hear by Thursday, and I'll share it then"), and acknowledge what's confidential rather than dodging the question. Drawing a clear line between fact and rumour lowers the cognitive load on your team.
- **Re-anchor on the controllable.** Trying to plan six months out when the roadmap shifts weekly is an exercise in frustration. Shorten sprints to weekly goals, use the time to pay down tech debt if product direction is frozen, and ship small wins - continuous delivery is a powerful antidote to anxiety.
- **Protect the 1:1 as a place to process, not report.** During a reorg, 1:1s should pivot away from status updates. Ask open-ended questions, listen without immediately defending the company's decisions, and watch for team members who suddenly go quiet - withdrawal is a common response to a threat state.
- **Manage your own battery.** Holding space for your team's anxiety while managing your own is exhausting. Find a peer network of other EMs, set communication boundaries around late-night Slack, and keep a log of your decisions and reasoning - it clears your head and gives you a record of how you handled it.

When a major shift lands, don't wait for your regular weekly meeting. Host a dedicated sync: name the change directly, share the three buckets of known, unknown, and confidential, define exactly what the team should focus on for the next five working days, leave real time for questions, and commit to a recurring update cadence so nobody's left in the dark.

## The bottom line

The fog will eventually clear. Roadmaps stabilise, new structures solidify, a new normal emerges. But your team will never forget how you treated them while they were lost in it. Shield them from noise, respect their intelligence with transparent communication, and give them stable ground to stand on, and you emerge with a high-trust, fiercely loyal team.

### Your next step

If a shift just landed on your team, don't wait for the next standup. Book a dedicated sync this week, and walk in with your three buckets already sorted: what's confirmed, what's genuinely unknown, and what you can't share yet. Clarity, even partial clarity, beats silence every time.
