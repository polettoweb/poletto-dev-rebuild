The industry is quietly optimising away its own future, one quarter at a time.

Here's the logic, and it's seductive: a senior engineer with AI now outproduces a senior engineer *plus* a junior - faster, cheaper, less overhead. So teams hire fewer juniors. Some have stopped entirely. Every individual decision looks rational.

Add them all up, and we're dismantling the machine that manufactures senior engineers. And almost nobody is treating that as a leadership problem - which is exactly what it is.

## What juniors were actually learning

We tell ourselves juniors did the "grunt work" - the boilerplate, the simple tickets, the tedious glue code. AI does that now, instantly and often better. Case closed.

Except the grunt work was never really about the output. It was the **apprenticeship**. Writing that boilerplate is how a junior learned where systems actually break, why a "simple" change had six side effects, what a codebase feels like when it's fighting you. AI can do the typing. It cannot do the *learning*. When you hand the reps to a machine, the code still gets written - but nobody grows.

## The trap: rational every quarter, ruinous every decade

This is a textbook local-optimum trap. Per quarter, the math is real: fewer juniors, more AI leverage, better delivery numbers. The cost doesn't appear on this year's P&L.

It shows up in five to seven years, as a senior-engineer shortage that money can't quickly fix - because seniors aren't hired, they're *grown*, slowly, out of juniors. **You cannot hire your way out of a shortage you created by refusing to train anyone.** And nearly everyone's plan is to poach ready-made seniors from someone else. When "let another company train them" is *everyone's* strategy, nobody trains them.

## The cruel inversion

AI didn't just remove the junior's work - it *inverted* it. The new junior job isn't writing simple code. It's **reviewing and verifying** AI-generated code - which is cognitively harder than writing it, a burden I've called the [Code Review Tax](/blog/managing-through-ai-fatigue-keeping-dev-teams-grounded-in-the-age-of-automation/), because you have to reconstruct an intent that was never there in the first place. We've handed juniors the *hard* job while removing the *easy on-ramp* that used to build that judgment. We took away the training wheels and pointed them downhill.

## This is a leadership problem, not a tooling one

None of this is the AI's fault, and none of it is inevitable. Every "we'll hire fewer juniors this year" is a **leadership decision** - usually made by optimising a metric that has nothing to say about where your senior engineers will come from in 2032. That's a strategic blind spot: a per-quarter number quietly setting a decade-long talent strategy, with no one in the room owning the trade-off.

## What leaders should actually do

You don't fix this by banning AI or romanticising boilerplate. You fix it by redesigning the apprenticeship for the world we're actually in:

- **Treat junior hiring as R&D for your senior bench**, not this quarter's output. Your future staff engineers are in today's early-career cohort, or they're nowhere.
- **Redesign how juniors learn, deliberately.** Replace osmosis with intentional practice: juniors dissecting AI output *alongside* a senior, owning small end-to-end problems where they have to think rather than prompt.
- **Give them problems just above the AI's competence.** Growth happens at the edge of ability. If a model can one-shot the task, it teaches nothing.
- **Make senior-pipeline health a metric you actually track** - like [management debt](/blog/refactoring-the-organization-how-to-pay-down-management-debt/), it's invisible right up until it's a crisis. Are juniors reaching mid-level? Is anyone growing into staff? A team that only ever hires seniors is running on borrowed time.

## The bottom line

AI is the best leverage engineering has had in a generation, and I'm not arguing against it. I'm arguing that leverage on a *shrinking base* is a trap. The teams that win the next decade won't be the ones that adopted AI fastest. They'll be the ones that used the leverage AI gave them to **grow people faster** - instead of deciding they no longer needed to.

### Your next step

Look at your last four engineering hires. How many were junior or early-career? If the honest answer is zero, you're not running lean - you're eating your seed corn, and the bill is just deferred.
