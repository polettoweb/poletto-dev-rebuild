I started a computer science degree with the Open University in 2016. I finished it in 2026. It should have taken six years part time. It took ten.

I'm still waiting for the final result as I write this, and I'm expecting an upper second. For years I only got distinctions, so that's a compromise. I'm fine with it, and the reason I'm fine with it is most of this story.

## Why a degree, when I already had the job

In 2016 I was a software engineer at fast-paced companies in the UK, at the beginning of my [second career](/blog/ive-retrained-my-career-twice-what-i-tell-engineers-worried-about-ai/). I had come from driving trains, through two bootcamps.

I wanted to become a good engineer. Learning the trade on the job is important, but so are good foundations, and the job wasn't going to give me those. So I signed up for a computer science degree alongside it.

## It took many shapes

At the start I studied on the commute. I had two hours a day on the train, plus weekends and holidays. The former train driver, now studying as a passenger.

When I had remote jobs, study became more fluid, fitted into the day and the evening around work.

Then I paused. It was meant to be a break, and it became a stop.

## I did quit

For four years I didn't study at all: my last two years in the UK, and my first two in the Netherlands. That's why it took ten years instead of six.

It was a lot, and my career was progressing well without it. I started to doubt the point of the degree. I tried changing course, first to a variation with design, then to one with business. Fortunately most of the modules transferred. But every time, I came back to computer science.

When I picked it up again, it was mostly weekends and holidays, because work was becoming more and more involving and the pressure kept rising.

## The pandemic, the burnout, and a business module

Then the pandemic struck, and everything became more difficult. [My burnout came first](/blog/burnout-from-the-inside-what-it-taught-me-as-a-manager/).

To be honest, the degree helped. When I couldn't focus on work, because my brain wasn't functioning, I was studying modules that had very little to do with software development. At the time it was a business management module, and it helped me shape the idea of moving into management.

So the degree I started to become a better engineer is part of the reason I stopped being one.

## Then my daughter was born

After the burnout, my daughter was born, and everything changed drastically.

Energy was very low and sleep was a luxury. The job was still the job: high pressure, high delivery, high expectations. Whenever I had thirty minutes free, I used them for university. I studied in the gaps, wherever I could find them.

The method had to change, and so did the expectations. Until then I had only got distinctions. After that I started to be OK with second-class passes.

**Reaching the goal became more important than reaching it with a gold medal.**

What got me through the hardest part was my will to finish things. I made it to the end, with the grade compromise.

## What it gave me

For most of my career I was the only person in the room without a degree. Not that anyone knew. Very few people asked, and they were surprised when I mentioned I was studying, sometimes while I was their manager. I've been lucky to work with very good people, professionals who know that knowledge and skills aren't directly connected to titles or degrees.

The real change was in my self-esteem. Not because of the degree itself, but because of the sense of accomplishment in finishing a project this big. I reached the end. I did it, with my own strength.

Some of the modules have stayed with me. Data analysis with AI is one of them, alongside algorithms and maths. I discovered that I like maths, which makes me laugh, because I hated it in high school.

## What I do for people studying on my teams

I don't just support engineers who are studying. I actively encourage it.

I often adjust a single engineer's quarter plan to make room for external learning. If someone tells me they have a module, an exam or a certification coming up that quarter, I help them stay away from stretch goals and shape their OKRs so they can succeed at both, the company work and the study.

## Would I recommend it today?

Yes, I would definitely recommend a part-time degree. But I'd suggest something different from what you already do every day, something that will help you grow in the future. Psychology applied to user experience, for example, or a data-related degree. Maybe project management, or how to talk with non-technical people.

You already learn your trade by doing it every day. A degree is where you can build the foundations, or the skills, that the daily job won't give you.

## The bottom line

A part-time degree alongside a demanding job is not one project. It's a series of projects in different shapes, with breaks, detours and compromises. Mine took ten years, a four-year stop, two changes of direction, a burnout and a newborn. It ended with a compromise on the grade, and the accomplishment of reaching the end.

### Your next step

If you manage engineers, ask them this week whether anyone is studying something alongside work, or wants to. If someone is, look at their plan for this quarter together and take out the stretch goal that collides with their exam.

If you're the one thinking about studying, pick the subject your daily job will never teach you.
