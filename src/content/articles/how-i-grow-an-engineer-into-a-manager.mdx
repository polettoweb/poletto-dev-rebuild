The best engineer on your team is not, by default, your next manager.

We all nod at that. Then a manager leaves, there's a gap on the org chart, and the strongest senior engineer is "obviously ready" - so we hand them the team as a reward for being excellent at a completely different job. That's how you lose a great engineer and gain a struggling manager in a single decision.

This is [the other half](/blog/what-i-look-for-when-hiring-and-growing-engineering-managers/) of how I evaluate managers: not how I spot the signals, but how I actually grow someone into the role. The promotion is the *last* step, not the first. Here's what comes before it.

## Promotion is a transfer, not a trophy

The most damaging idea in our industry is that management is the level above senior engineer. It isn't. It's a different profession that happens to share a domain - like a great surgeon being made hospital administrator. When the only way someone can grow their scope or salary is to stop engineering, you keep minting resentful managers who took the job because it was the door marked "up," not because they wanted it.

So the first thing I check isn't the candidate. It's the ladder. If your Staff/Principal track isn't real - real scope, real money, real status - every "promotion" into management is partly coerced. Fix the IC ladder first. Then management becomes a genuine choice.

## Try the job before you take the job

The highest-signal thing I can do is give someone real management surface area *before* any title is on the table: owning the onboarding of the next new hire end to end, running the design-review process for a quarter, mentoring a junior through a genuinely hard stretch, chairing the retro and owning the follow-through.

Then I watch. Does it energise them or drain them? When someone else ships the thing, do they light up or feel a flicker of "but I could have done it faster"? The title should *confirm* a transition that has already started - not gamble on one that might.

## The real work is the withdrawal

Everyone thinks the hard part is learning to run 1:1s. It isn't. The hard part is an identity shift: **your value is no longer what you ship.**

New managers relapse into IC work under pressure - grabbing the scary ticket, taking the fragile service back - because that's where they feel competent. I call it *the withdrawal*: the pull back to the keyboard whenever leadership feels ambiguous and impossible to measure by lunchtime. So I name it out loud, early and often: there will be weeks where you feel like you did nothing - no commits, just conversations. That feeling isn't failure. That feeling *is the job*.

## Guardrails, not scripts

I don't hand new managers a playbook for every situation. I give them the principles - optimise for the team's output, create clarity, hold the technical bar, develop people - and room to find their own style inside them. Teams can smell a borrowed persona instantly, and the only way someone finds their authentic version of the job is if you resist the urge to script it for them.

## Protect them from becoming the critical path

The fastest way to sink a promising new manager is to let their old team keep leaning on them as the best engineer in the room. So I actively redistribute that dependency - reassign the service only they understand, spread the context deliberately. Yes, it slows delivery for a sprint or two. That's the price of the transition, and it's cheap compared to losing the person.

## Let them fail - small, and on purpose

Growth needs real stakes: a difficult underperformance conversation, a scope cut they have to sell, a tense stakeholder to manage. Then I calibrate the distance - close enough to catch them if it goes badly, far enough that the outcome is genuinely theirs. Rescuing them teaches nothing; abandoning them is cruel. The entire craft of growing a leader lives in that gap.

## Keep the exit ramp open

This is the part almost everyone skips, and it's the one that makes all the rest work: **make it genuinely safe to go back.** A new manager who can't say "this isn't for me" without it feeling like a public failure is trapped - and trapped managers quietly make everyone around them miserable. So I say it explicitly, up front: if management isn't for you, returning to an IC or Staff track is a *success* of the experiment, not a failure of the person.

## The bottom line

Growing a manager is slow, unglamorous, deeply human work. You're not levelling someone up a ladder - you're helping them change careers without losing their footing, and giving them a way back if it turns out not to fit.

### Your next step

Have someone who's "obviously ready" for management? Don't promote them yet. This month, hand them one real slice of the job - own an onboarding, run the design reviews, carry a hard mentoring relationship - and watch whether it lights them up or wears them down. Let the title confirm what the work already told you.
