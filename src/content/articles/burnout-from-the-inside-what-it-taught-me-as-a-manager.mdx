It was a stand-up like any other. A contracting assignment, a complex codebase, and tasks handed to me after a single day, with no onboarding. Expectations were high, the culture was poor and the air in the room was tense.

I couldn't focus. I couldn't understand what they were asking me.

Inside me a voice was shouting: *Stop. This is not what you want or need.*

In Italian, *la voce* is feminine, so in my head the voice has always been a she. Things had been going downhill for a couple of months. That morning was her last scream.

I've written before about [changing career twice](/blog/ive-retrained-my-career-twice-what-i-tell-engineers-worried-about-ai/), and about the contracting period that ended in burnout. I gave it one section there. This is the longer version, and what it changed in how I manage people.

## How it got there

The pandemic had just started. A consultancy had cancelled my contract one week before I was due to start, then came back six months later and hired me. They placed me on a temporary assignment with a client.

I was not a member of that team. I was a machine for working through tickets. I don't know whether anyone noticed anything was wrong. Nobody there was looking at me as a person. It was a very impersonal job, and a strange experience I don't want to repeat.

After that stand-up I spoke with my manager at the consultancy and resigned. About two days later I was gone.

## She said rest. I couldn't.

What the voice wanted was simple. Rest. Stop focusing on problems. Let go, relax, think about nothing for a while.

I couldn't. Life in the Netherlands is expensive, and a few months without work build up on the bills very quickly. So I did the thing you are not supposed to do: I tried to recover while still working.

It didn't go well. For a while I couldn't stay in a role for more than six to nine months. My concentration was close to non-existent, and every task looked like a mountain I had no way of climbing.

## Same requirements, different job

Looking back, my mistake wasn't working too hard. It was ignoring the voice, and taking jobs that didn't resonate with me.

I focused on the role and not on the actual work. I believed I could function in any job whose requirements I matched. Nothing could be more wrong. Two jobs with the same title and the same list of skills can be completely different places to spend your days.

I like working with people. I like working for companies that care about people. I like caring about growth, and I need to avoid work that is impersonal and transactional. None of that is in a job description, and all of it decided whether I could function.

**Some jobs suit us and some jobs we should avoid. Ideals and attitude matter as much as skills.**

## The way out was a different job

What got me out was not a holiday or a technique. It was becoming a manager.

By then I couldn't code any more. Coding had started to feel aseptic and mechanical to me. And I had always wanted to focus more on people and growth than on code. Moving into management was the first time in years the work matched what the voice had been asking for.

It took a couple of years to get back to 80 or 90 percent of my old self. Now, almost seven years later, I am very close, 98 or 99 percent. Maybe I will never be fully there again. But I am very close.

## What I see in others now

Having been through it, I see the signals in other people more often. Not always, but often. They are rarely dramatic:

- **Attention slips.** Someone who used to follow every thread starts losing them, or asks for things to be repeated.
- **Anger or negativity.** Small things provoke reactions that are out of proportion, or everything becomes a reason things won't work.
- **Interest drops on non-critical work.** The critical tasks still get done, so delivery looks fine. Everything around them, the reviews, the side discussions, the small improvements, quietly stops.

That last one is the easiest to miss, because the dashboards stay green.

When I see them, I approach the person with care and empathy and ask if everything is OK. I don't force anything. I give them the chance to open up, if they want to.

That only works if there is trust already. They have to believe you are there for them, not there to push them. You can't build that in the conversation where you need it.

**If they open up**, we design a way of working that fits what they're going through, either within the team or together with HR, depending on the situation.

**If they don't**, I volunteer some changes to their day anyway. I shift some work or a tough topic to someone else, and give them something lighter: less stressful, or with less of a spotlight on it at the moment. The aim is a bit of rest, in a way that's still compatible with what the team has to deliver.

This is the strength of a great team: they are all there for each other. In this industry, under this kind of pressure, it rotates. Today it's one person, tomorrow it could be you. Helping others when they need it makes it easier to be helped when you do.

## Onboarding is work

The thing I changed most deliberately is onboarding, because that stand-up is the opposite of what I want anyone on my teams to go through.

I give people time to onboard, and plenty of support while they do. Not just documentation: real people, with real empathy, to learn from. Onboarding should be a good experience, not a stressful step to get through before the real work starts. **Onboarding is work already.** I've written about [how I design an onboarding plan](/blog/designing-an-effective-onboarding-plan-for-new-engineers/), and this is the reason behind it.

## Leave the machine work to the machines

Being treated as a machine for working through tickets is what burned me out. It's worth saying now, because that framing is getting more common as agents start closing tickets too.

I think AI is perfect for impersonal, mechanical work. Let it have that. What we should keep for humans is the critical part: onboarding, team building, personal and professional support, and accountability. No AI can do those.

## The bottom line

Burnout didn't arrive in one day. It arrived over months, while I ignored a voice that knew what I needed before I did. What got me out wasn't resting harder, it was finding work that fitted who I am. Now, as a manager, my job is to notice when someone else's voice is getting loud, and to make sure nobody on my teams starts the way I did at that stand-up.

### Your next step

Think about the newest person on your team. Were they given real work before they had a real person to ask? Then think about someone who has been quieter lately. Are they still doing the critical work but nothing around it? Either way, have a conversation this week that isn't about tickets.
