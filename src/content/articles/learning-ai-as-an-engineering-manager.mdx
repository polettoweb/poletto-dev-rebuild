The first AI tool I ever used was GitHub Copilot's autocomplete, back when I was still a developer. It finished my lines of code, sometimes well and sometimes badly, and I treated it like a clever spellchecker.

I've been a manager for a while now, and the conversation around AI has changed completely. Every week brings a new model, a new agent framework, a new way of working you're apparently already late for. The pressure, spoken or not, is to learn all of it, now.

I'm not doing that. I'm learning AI the same way I've learned most things in my career: on the job, one real problem at a time. It's slower. I think it's also the only way that sticks when your calendar isn't yours.

## Where I started

For a long time I used AI as a sparring partner for the day-to-day parts of the job. A first review of a document, a flow or a procedure before a human saw it. Gathering information on something I didn't know yet. A sanity check on a decision. Pointing me to where I should look to learn something properly.

That was genuinely useful, and it's still most of what I use it for. But I was asking it questions, not working with it. Whatever came back was only as good as my question, and I had no way of knowing what it didn't know.

## Learn as you go

The method I've settled on has three layers.

- **Foundations first.** The basics that help with every task: how these models work, what they are good and bad at, how to give them context and how to check what comes back. This part is general, and it's worth learning once, properly.
- **Then the company's own knowledge.** Before I ask anything specific, I put the relevant internal documentation into the context. A generic answer about incident response is easy to find. An answer that fits how *this* organisation actually works is not.
- **Then one area at a time.** With the documentation in place, I zoom into the part I'm working on: incidents, quality, observability, organisational growth. For that area I learn the best practices, the format people expect, which sources and data it needs, and what an acceptable output looks like.

Learning while working is slower than trying to learn everything at once. You won't be the person who can talk about every new release. In exchange you learn by doing, which has always been how I learn best, and you get good at real cases instead of theory and hypothetical future uses.

It also keeps the learning honest. If a technique doesn't help with an actual problem on my desk, it waits.

## The gap document

The moment I understood what AI can and can't do for a manager came while I was rebuilding one of our company processes.

I asked my LLM to collect everything it could find about the process and produce a gap document: a list of every issue and gap the process currently had. It came back quickly, well structured and confident.

Then I did the same exercise the old way. I gathered the relevant stakeholders and asked them the same questions, each one limited to their own area of involvement and expertise.

Comparing the two was the lesson. Wherever the documentation had holes, the model had filled them with assumptions. They read just as convincingly as the parts that were true. Some of them simply couldn't work, and I only knew that because I knew the process and the people who ran it.

**AI is only as good as its sources, whether that's your prompt or your documentation.** The gaps in your documentation become gaps in your AI, except now they come back looking like answers.

That changed how I use it. The AI's gap document is now my starting point for the stakeholder conversations, not a replacement for them. It tells me where to ask, and the people tell me what's true. It's the same rule I ended up with when [an agent rebuilt this site](/blog/the-agent-wrote-the-code-leading-it-was-the-job/): don't take the claim, take the receipt.

## No protected time

The hardest part of learning as a manager isn't difficulty. It's time.

Part of the job is to be there when a problem appears, and problems don't send calendar invites. A morning blocked for learning is a morning that will be interrupted, and after the third time you stop blocking it.

So I stopped trying to protect time and started using the unexpected free time instead. A meeting that ends early. A quiet hour after an incident is closed. To make that work, I split learning into small pieces that I can drop and pick up again without losing much: one concept, one experiment, one document to try against one question.

Feeling like a beginner again has never been the problem. I've done it many times, and twice drastically: [from trains to code, and from code to management](/blog/ive-retrained-my-career-twice-what-i-tell-engineers-worried-about-ai/). The other thing that prepared me was finishing a computing degree with the Open University while working a demanding full-time job, through the birth of my daughter, a pandemic and the start of the AI revolution. It was absolutely hard, and rewarding at the end. It taught me that learning in fragments is still learning, as long as the fragments keep coming.

## What I'd tell another engineering manager

**Focus on people.** They are, and will always be, the centre of everything we do. Right now AI looks like the only thing that matters. The dot-com boom felt like that, and so did crypto. Technologies come and go. The people in your teams don't.

**Use AI, but learn what you need, not everything.** Pick the problem in front of you and learn the part of AI that helps with it. Breadth will come by itself.

**Learn by doing.** A real task with real documentation teaches you more in an afternoon than a week of tutorials about use cases you may never have.

**Ask for help, especially from your engineers.** They will appreciate being asked, and you will learn a lot. Being smart about this is their job, and most of them are doing it amazingly well. It's also the cheapest way I know to stay [technically current as a manager](/blog/technically-updating-yourself-while-being-an-engineering-manager/) without pretending you're still an IC.

## The bottom line

You don't need to become an AI expert to lead well through this change. You need to be good at using it for the work you actually do, and honest about where it stops being reliable. Learning as you go gets you there slower than cramming, but you arrive with skills that fit your real job instead of a list of tools you once tried.

### Your next step

Pick one task you'll do this week anyway: a process document, an incident review, a proposal. Do it with AI, with the relevant documentation in the context. Then check the result with one person who knows that area well, and write down every place where the AI filled a gap with an assumption. That list is your learning plan for next week, and probably a documentation backlog too.
