Most engineering management books fall into one of two traps: dense leadership theory with nowhere to put it on a Monday morning, or technical war stories with nothing to say about the actual, unglamorous work of running a team. There aren't many written in plain language for someone who's brand new to the role and just needs to know what to do next.

*Engineering Manager for the Rest of Us* by Sarah Drasner is the exception, and it's the book I recommend most to first-time managers precisely because it skips the jargon and the grand theory in favour of practical, human advice grounded in what the job actually looks like day to day.

A few of the takeaways that stuck with me:

- **Communication is the actual job, not a soft add-on to it.** Connecting technical teams with everyone else, setting expectations, giving feedback, agreeing on goals together - this is the work, not a distraction from it.
- **Understand the people, not just the technology.** Knowing what drives your engineers and what problems they're actually facing matters more than knowing the stack. A team does its best work when people feel good and work well together, and that's built deliberately, not assumed.
- **Hiring and retention are a leadership skill, not an HR function.** A great team doesn't happen by accident - it's a deliberate investment in finding the right people and building a place where they can grow.
- **Set goals your team can actually see themselves in.** Goals that match the company's mission but ignore your team's actual strengths and gaps produce compliance, not motivation.
- **Feedback has to be a system, not an event.** Regular, specific, and tied to real growth - not a once-a-year form filled in under deadline pressure.
- **Resolve conflict quickly, not eventually.** Left alone, small team friction doesn't resolve itself; it compounds into something that shows up in retention numbers months later.

There are more lessons in the book than these, and probably a few that will matter more to you than they did to me - that's the point of a book written for a wide range of first-time managers rather than one narrow career story.

### Your next step

If you're new to management and every book on your shelf feels like it's written in a language you haven't learned yet, start here. Pick one lesson from the list above - the one that names a gap you already know you have - and try it with your team this week, not the whole list at once.
