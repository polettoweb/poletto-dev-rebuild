For most of my career, building software was a relay. A product manager decided what to build, a designer shaped it, engineers built it, and somebody tested it. Every handoff lost a bit of context, and a big part of an engineering manager's job was keeping the baton from being dropped.

AI is shortening that relay. One person can now go from a customer problem to a prototype, and often to a shipped feature, without handing anything over. The industry is starting to call these people product builders. Some of them are engineers who learned to think like PMs. More and more of them are PMs who learned to build.

I think this is one of the best things AI is doing to our industry. It also changes what it means to lead a team, and I don't think most organisations have caught up yet.

## Everyone can build. That's not the hard part anymore

When building was expensive, the cost itself acted as a filter. You couldn't build everything, so you had to choose. Roadmaps, prioritisation and long arguments about scope were all, in a way, ways of rationing engineering time.

When building gets cheap, that filter disappears. The risk is no longer that you can't build something. It's that you build everything: ten half-finished features, three ways of doing the same thing, a product that grows faster than anyone can explain it.

So the scarce skill moves. It's no longer "can you build it?" It's "should this exist, what does good look like, and when do we stop?" That's judgement, and judgement doesn't come from a model. I made a similar point about strategy in [engineering strategy is mostly saying no](/blog/engineering-strategy-is-mostly-saying-no/). With AI, saying no becomes a daily skill for everyone on the team, not just a quarterly one for leadership.

## What changes for the manager

If your team is made of product builders, coordinating handoffs is a much smaller part of the job. Three things grow instead.

- **Coaching judgement.** Helping people decide what's worth building, and when a prototype has answered its question and should be thrown away. This is closer to coaching a product manager than to managing a sprint.
- **Holding the quality line.** When one person owns an idea end to end, nobody downstream catches their mistakes. The manager's job is to make sure the [guardrails are in place](/blog/ai-made-code-cheap-quality-is-still-expensive/) so speed doesn't quietly turn into fragility.
- **Keeping the product coherent.** Many people building fast in parallel can produce something that feels like many products. Someone has to look across the whole thing and ask whether it still makes sense to a customer.

None of this is less work than before. It's different work, and it's much more about people than about process.

## The human side of a role change

Roles shifting like this is hard on people, and it's easy to forget that while talking about productivity.

Some engineers feel they're being asked to become PMs, without the training or the title. Some PMs feel their job is being absorbed by people who can build. Career ladders written for the relay don't describe the product builder at all, so people can't see how to grow.

A good manager makes this transition explicit. Talk about it openly. Update the career framework so it rewards judgement and ownership, not just output. Give engineers time to learn product thinking and PMs time to learn the engineering basics. And accept that not everyone wants to become a product builder. Deep specialists still matter, and a team made only of generalists has its own blind spots.

## Why this is the kind of team I want to lead

I've [retrained my career twice](/blog/ive-retrained-my-career-twice-what-i-tell-engineers-worried-about-ai/), so I'm not attached to job titles. What I care about is whether a team builds the right things well, and whether the people on it are growing.

That's why this shift excites me. I'd be very happy to lead a team where some people came from product and some from engineering, as long as they share the same habits: start from the customer problem, build to learn, and own the result. And as the roles blur, I find myself just as interested in the product side of the work as in the engineering side. A manager of product builders has to be a bit of a product person too.

## The bottom line

AI made building cheap, which makes choosing the hard part. Teams of product builders can move faster than any relay ever did, but only if someone helps them choose well, keeps quality in place and looks after the people whose roles are changing. That's still a manager's job. It just looks more like product leadership and coaching than it used to.

### Your next step

Look at your team's last month of work and ask, for each thing that shipped: who decided it should exist, and when? If the answer is often "nobody really, it was easy to build", your team doesn't have a building problem. It has a choosing problem, and that's the conversation worth having next.
