After stepping into engineering management in the Netherlands myself, I found it surprisingly difficult to decide whether to stay technical or go full leadership. Here's what I've learned, and what I think others should consider.

As engineers rise into management, they hit a fork: stay rooted in technology, sharpening depth while guiding others from the trenches, or lean fully into people and organisational leadership, where strategy and cross-functional alignment dominate the job. In the Netherlands, home to a thriving and mature tech ecosystem, this question is especially relevant - the market supports both paths, but there's little room for ambiguity. **You're expected to choose.**

## A booming market with clear career tracks

The Dutch tech sector is booming, and Amsterdam consistently ranks among Europe's top tech hubs, with Eindhoven, Rotterdam, and Utrecht gaining ground. Dutch companies, local and international - ASML, Adyen, TomTom, Booking.com - tend to offer structured career ladders inspired by Silicon Valley standards: IC tracks that go up to Staff and Principal Engineer, and management tracks that scale to Head of Engineering, VP, and CTO.

## The fork in the road

The **technical track** lets you grow as a technologist: architectural decisions, mentoring, code quality, high-impact projects, without managing people. The Staff Engineer model - engineering excellence that doesn't require leaving the keyboard - is increasingly visible here; companies like bol.com, MessageBird, and Picnic actively support it with compensation and status on par with management.

The **management track** shifts you toward hiring, performance management, cross-functional collaboration, budgeting, and organisational politics. As you climb - Engineering Manager, Head of Engineering, Director, VP - your influence over strategy grows and technical involvement fades. In most Dutch companies, EMs aren't expected to code regularly once they're managing more than six to eight people; your primary deliverable becomes organisational health and delivery efficiency, not systems design.

## Why Dutch engineers must choose

Dutch work culture values directness, pragmatism, and a focus on results - trying to juggle people management and deep technical work is generally frowned upon, because it dilutes both. Hybrid "tech lead manager" roles exist, but they're usually transitional; once you're managing multiple squads, staying technical often becomes impractical. That's not a failure, it's structural - engineering leadership here demands operational maturity, not just architectural fluency.

It also cuts the other way: in some markets engineers get nudged into management because it's the only way to "move up." Not here. Dutch companies increasingly recognise the value of senior ICs, and roles like Principal Engineer or Tech Lead carry influence that rivals management - substance over status.

## The risks of going full management

Once you're out of the codebase for six to twelve months, returning to the same level isn't easy - you may face scepticism from hiring managers or a real confidence gap if you try to pivot back. Management isn't just "soft skills" either: conflict resolution, career development, and org planning are a serious craft with high expectations. And the wins arrive slower and more indirectly than the dopamine of shipping something.

Before you choose, it's worth asking yourself a few honest questions: Do I get more satisfaction from solving technical puzzles, or from enabling people to succeed? Do I actually want to own hiring, performance reviews, and team culture? Can I let go of the codebase and still feel valuable? Am I comfortable stepping into politics and product trade-offs?

## The bottom line

In the Dutch tech ecosystem, you're not punished for staying technical, and you're not pushed into management to grow. Both tracks are respected and offer long-term career growth in a market as balanced and rational as this one. But sitting on the fence doesn't scale here.

### Your next step

Answer the four questions above honestly, on paper, this week - not for your manager, for yourself. Your answers should decide your next move, not the job title or the salary ladder attached to it.
