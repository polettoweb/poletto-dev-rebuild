The advice engineering managers usually get about staying technical is to carve out coding time - block a few hours a week, keep a side project alive, don't let the skills atrophy. It's well-meant, and for most managers it quietly fails within a month, because the hours it needs are the same hours your actual job is competing for.

The better approach isn't finding more solo coding time. It's using the job you already have differently.

## Learn from your team, deliberately

Your engineers almost certainly know more than you do about whatever's newest - and that's fine, it's not a threat to manage around. Ask people to present on a technique or tool they've been using and you get two things at once: you stay current, and you show the team their expertise is genuinely valued rather than merely tolerated. A hackathon or coding challenge does the same thing with more energy behind it.

## Treat conferences as more than a ticket

Conferences are only tedious if you attend them passively. Bring the team and make it a shared experience rather than a solo trip. The hallway conversations and after-parties are usually where the actually useful, unfiltered information lives - more than the talks themselves.

## Keep a small personal project alive, on your terms

You won't have hands-on coding time the way you did as an IC, but a small side project or an open-source contribution keeps your instincts sharp and gives you a low-stakes place to try something new. Keep expectations realistic - the point is staying sharp, not shipping the next big thing.

## Read with intent, not out of obligation

Tech blogs, release notes, and a handful of people worth following are enough to stay oriented without turning it into a second job. The goal is pattern recognition - knowing what's shifting and why - not deep expertise in every new framework that ships.

## Use online learning for the gaps you can actually name

Courses and webinars are most useful when they're aimed at a specific gap you've noticed in yourself, not a general sense that you should "keep learning." Narrow beats broad here.

## Go where the cross-functional knowledge already is

Your job isn't limited to the engineering team. Product, design, and QA all hold context you don't have, and the fastest way to understand a new technology's real impact is often through how another discipline is already using it.

## Mentor - it forces you to stay current

Mentoring someone is one of the more reliable ways to stay technically sharp, because you have to keep updated enough to answer their questions well. It's also one of the few management activities that gives something back in both directions.

### Your next step

Pick one of these that you're not already doing, and try it for a month rather than adding all seven at once. The manager version of staying technical isn't more solo hours at a keyboard - it's finding the technique that fits the job you actually have now.
